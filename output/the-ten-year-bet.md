# Post copy: The Ten-Year Bet

**Title:** Buffett's ten-year bet: the laziest portfolio vs. Wall Street's smartest money

**Description:**

In 2007, Warren Buffett bet $1 million that a plain S&P 500 index fund would beat five hand-picked funds of hedge funds over ten years, net of fees.

He wasn't forecasting the market. Before costs, active managers as a group earn the market's return, because together they are the market. After costs, they must trail it, and hedge funds charge in layers. Skill was uncertain. Costs were certain.

Year one, 2008: the market fell 37%, the hedge funds lost less, and Buffett was behind. He held. A ten-year bet is scored at year ten.

The stake taught a second lesson. By 2012, the "safe" Treasury bonds holding it would pay under 1% a year, so both sides moved it into Berkshire stock. Risk isn't volatility; it's the chance you miss your goal.

The result: the index fund gained 126% (about 8.5% a year). The five funds of funds gained between 3% and 88%. The winnings, about $2.2M, went to Girls Inc. of Omaha.

The fair counterpoint: one decade is one sample. But the arithmetic of costs holds in any decade.

"Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter."

Sources and fact-check:
- Results and the fund-of-funds endpoints: Buffett's 2017 letter to Berkshire shareholders
- The ~60% fee estimate and the "fees never falter" quote: Buffett's 2016 letter
- Stake, the 2012 switch to Berkshire, and the payout: Institutional Investor, "Protégé Partners Pays Up in Buffett Bet"
- Terms and the 2008 year: Validea; Investor Daily; The Long Now Foundation (Long Bets)
- Cost arithmetic: W. F. Sharpe, "The Arithmetic of Active Management" (1991)
Note: the widely quoted "7.1% vs 2.2% a year" are the nine-year figures through 2016.

Fund-of-funds yearly paths are illustrative between their true endpoints. The fee waterfall is illustrative. Educational content, not investment advice.
